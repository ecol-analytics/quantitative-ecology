# Ecological Models in Space and Time

## A five-day extension to Quantitative Ecology: From Research Design to Predictive Modelling

The core course develops a question-driven approach to ecological modelling, progressing from research design and exploratory analysis through statistical explanation, model structure and prediction.

This extension begins with the same modelling framework but expands the ecological domain. Observations that were initially treated as a snapshot are placed explicitly in space and time, allowing increasingly demanding questions about ecological structure, prediction, transferability and forecasting.

The broad progression is:

**Space → Time → Integration → Prediction → Forecasting**

The objective is not to teach spatial and temporal methods as separate statistical toolboxes. Each stage instead asks what additional structure a model must represent as the ecological question expands beyond the observations used to construct it.

***

## Day 6 — Ecology in space

### What changes when ecological observations have locations?

Ecological observations do not occur independently of geography. Their locations may reflect environmental gradients, sampling design, ecological processes and spatial dependence.

The first day of the extension takes models developed during the core course and asks what was lost when observations were treated simply as rows in a dataset.

Topics include:

* spatial extent, resolution, grain and support;

* coordinates and spatial data structures;

* mapping ecological observations;

* environmental surfaces and spatial covariates;

* spatial gradients and pattern;

* spatial dependence and autocorrelation;

* examining model residuals in space;

* representing spatial structure in ecological models.

### Central idea

**Where an observation was made may contain information that the model has ignored.**

The emphasis is not on producing maps, but on understanding how spatial structure changes the interpretation, assumptions and predictions of an ecological model.

***

## Day 7 — Ecology through time

### What changes when ecological observations have histories?

Ecological systems change through time. Observations may contain trends, seasonal structure, cycles, delayed responses and temporal dependence that are invisible when the data are treated as a static snapshot.

Participants extend the same modelling logic used for spatial structure to consider how ecological relationships develop through time.

Topics include:

* temporal extent and resolution;

* trends and long-term change;

* seasonality and cycles;

* temporal dependence and autocorrelation;

* repeated observations;

* ecological lags and delayed responses;

* changing environmental conditions;

* representing temporal structure in ecological models.

### Central idea

**When an observation was made may matter as much as what was measured.**

Temporal structure is treated as part of the ecological process rather than simply as a statistical complication.

***

## Day 8 — Integrating space and time

### How do ecological processes operate across both space and time?

Increasingly, ecological analyses combine observations collected across locations and years with environmental information derived from monitoring programmes, remote sensing, environmental models and other open data sources.

Combining these sources requires more than matching coordinates and dates. Biological observations and environmental measurements may represent very different spatial and temporal scales.

Topics include:

* ecological observations as functions of space and time;

* spatiotemporal environmental variation;

* spatial and temporal support;

* scale and resolution mismatch;

* linking biological observations to environmental data;

* aggregation and temporal windows;

* heterogeneous and open ecological datasets;

* missing coverage and uneven sampling;

* representing interacting spatial and temporal structure.

### Central idea

**Combining datasets means combining their spatial and temporal scales, supports and uncertainties.**

More data do not automatically provide more ecological information. Their provenance and structure determine what can reasonably be inferred from them.

***

## Day 9 — Prediction across domains

### Where and when should our understanding generalise?

The core course introduces prediction by asking whether a model can predict observations that were not used to construct it. Spatial and temporal data make that question more demanding.

A model may predict withheld observations from the same sampled system extremely well while performing poorly when transferred to another location, another period or different environmental conditions.

Topics include:

* defining the prediction domain;

* interpolation and extrapolation;

* spatial transfer;

* temporal transfer;

* environmental novelty;

* random and structured cross-validation;

* spatial and temporal blocking;

* transferability;

* evaluating predictions in relation to their intended use.

### Central idea

**A model should be validated for the domain in which it is intended to predict.**

Predictive performance is therefore not a property of a model in isolation. It depends on how far the prediction problem extends beyond the evidence used to construct the model.

***

## Day 10 — Forecasting ecological change

### What happens next?

Forecasting extends ecological prediction into a future that has not yet been observed. The response is unknown, future environmental conditions may themselves be uncertain, and relationships estimated from historical observations may not remain constant.

The final day considers what is required to move from explaining observed ecological patterns and predicting across known domains to making defensible statements about future ecological states.

Topics include:

* prediction versus forecasting;

* forecast horizons;

* future environmental conditions;

* uncertainty in predictors and responses;

* propagation of uncertainty;

* environmental novelty and extrapolation;

* stationarity and changing ecological relationships;

* scenarios and forecasts;

* updating models as new observations become available;

* communicating the limits of ecological forecasts.

### Central idea

**Forecasting moves the model beyond both the observed response and the observed future state of the system.**

The final question is therefore not simply whether a model can generate a future prediction, but what evidence supports that prediction, how uncertainty grows with distance from the observations, and what new information would most improve the forecast.

***

# Extension progression

Across the five days, the ecological domain progressively expands:

**Where were the observations made?**

↓

**When were they made?**

↓

**How are space and time connected?**

↓

**Where and when can the model predict?**

↓

**What can the model say about a future that has not yet been observed?**

The extension therefore continues the central principle of the core course: quantitative methods follow from ecological questions.

The objective is not to accumulate increasingly specialised techniques, but to understand what additional information and assumptions are required as ecological inference moves progressively further from the observations on which it is based.
